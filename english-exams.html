The golden rule. In option-based tasks, never start with the options. The options are full of distractors and misleading wording. Start with the text. This applies to Matching Headings and Matching Features. It is deliberately broken for True/False and Yes/No, where the answers are ordered.

1. Reading is not Listening

  • In Reading the text is in front of you. In Listening it is not.
  • In Listening you hear the recording once. In the Cambridge exams you can listen to the text more than once, so the pressure is different.
  • Because of this, in Listening you are more dependent on the questions; in Reading the text is your starting point.
  • But the two skills are not opposites. Most question types give you options, and in all of them the options are a trap if you read them first.

2. Matching Headings and Matching Features are unordered

  • The answers can be anywhere in the text, so you cannot work through them in sequence.
  • Both start with the text: survey the layout, skim the first and last sentence of each paragraph, decide which paragraphs are relevant.

Matching Headings

  • Skim, then go to the headings, underline keywords and eliminate the headings done for you. There are more headings than you need.
  • The topic sentence is not always first. Do not stop at the first and last sentence if the main idea is not there — keep reading.
  • Just because Matching Headings asks you mainly to skim, that does not mean you should stop reading the rest of the paragraph if necessary. Keep reading, because the heading must cover the whole paragraph — one word or one sentence is not enough.

Matching Features

  • After skimming, think of synonyms before you go back to the text, because the text will paraphrase, not repeat your keywords.
  • There are fewer features than paragraphs, and one feature may match more than one paragraph. This is why the instruction says you may use a letter more than once.
  • Features ask for connections between facts and opinions — who said or found what. That is why you need to close read around the keywords, not just scan.

3. True/False and Yes/No break the rule — on purpose

  • The answers are in the same order as the information in the text. You are on safe ground reading the statements first.
  • Read the statement, underline keywords, think of synonyms, turn it into a question, skim the paragraph, scan for the keywords, then close read around them.
  • Both True/False and Yes/No ask you to look for synonyms, just as Matching Features does. The text will paraphrase, not repeat your keywords.
  • Looking for specific information does not mean you can skip close reading. It is where the traps live.
  • True/False tests factual information in the text. Yes/No tests the writer's views or claims. Same mechanics, different target.
  • Reporting a view is not the same as agreeing with it. If the writer reports someone else's claim and gives no opinion, the answer is Not Given, not Yes or No.

Reading Passage: The Unexpected Benefits of Boredom

Read once for the general idea before attempting the tasks below.

ABoredom is one of the most disliked emotions. We avoid it with smartphones, streaming services and constant activity. Yet a growing body of research suggests that boredom may not be the enemy we assume. Psychologists are beginning to argue that boredom can serve a purpose, and that our attempts to eliminate it may be counterproductive. This does not mean that all boredom is good, but it does mean that the emotion deserves a more careful look.

BIn one well-known experiment, Dr. Elena Marsh and her colleagues asked participants to spend fifteen minutes copying numbers from a telephone directory. A second group was given a more interesting task. Afterwards, both groups were asked to suggest as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups. The participants who had completed the boring task produced significantly more ideas, and their ideas were rated as more creative by independent judges. Marsh concluded that boredom can create a mental “empty space” that encourages the mind to wander and generate novel connections.

CProfessor David Chen takes a different angle. He argues that boredom is a signal, like hunger or thirst, that something is missing. When we feel bored, we are motivated to seek new goals and experiences. This can be productive, but Chen warns that it can also lead to risk-taking. People who are bored may seek stimulation through gambling, unsafe driving or other dangerous behaviours. For Chen, boredom is neither good nor bad in itself; its effects depend on how people respond to it.

DDr. Priya Nair has studied boredom in schools. She argues that modern education has over-filled students’ days with structured activities, leaving little room for the kind of unstructured time that allows creativity to develop. Nair does not suggest that schools should abandon structured learning. Rather, she believes that students should be given more freedom to manage their own time, including periods when they are allowed to be bored. In her view, the outcome of boredom depends heavily on the context in which it occurs.

ETaken together, these findings suggest that boredom is a more complex emotion than we usually assume. It can spark creativity, motivate change and, in some circumstances, lead to harm. The writer’s view is that society has been too quick to dismiss boredom as a purely negative experience. What matters most is not boredom itself but how we respond to it: whether we use it as a prompt to reflect and explore, or simply as a discomfort to be eliminated as quickly as possible.
